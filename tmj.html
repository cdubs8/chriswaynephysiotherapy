What is a TMJ disorder?

Temporomandibular disorders (TMD), often called TMJ disorders, are problems with the jaw joint, the muscles that move it and the structures around it. You have one temporomandibular joint (TMJ) on each side of your face, where your jawbone meets your skull.

TMD can have many causes, including clenching or grinding, stress, an injury to the jaw or neck, arthritis, or changes in how the jaw and neck move together. Because the cause is different for each person, your treatment should be too.

Who is this for? Anyone with new or persistent jaw and related issues.

Symptoms we treat

TMD can show up well beyond the jaw. Common symptoms include:

Jaw

  • Pain, tension or stiffness
  • Uneven jaw movements, abnormal bite or clicking
  • Clenching or grinding
  • Popping or locking

Spinal

  • Neck and back pain, tension or stiffness
  • Forward head posture

Ear, nose & throat

  • Ear aching, blocking or ringing
  • Swallowing difficulties
  • Facial pain or pressure
  • Sleeping difficulties

Neurological

  • Headaches
  • Light-headedness
  • Brain fog

TMJ questions

Do I need a referral?

No. You can book directly with us. If you are claiming through insurance, check with your provider first in case they have their own requirements.

How many visits will I need?

On average, people with a new injury need 5 to 6 visits, and people with a chronic injury need 6 to 12. It depends on the cause and how long you have had symptoms. You will get an honest estimate after your first visit, and we review it as you improve.

Will treatment be painful?

Most people find treatment comfortable. Some techniques can cause mild, short-lived soreness. We always explain what to expect and adjust to what feels right for you.

Do you treat tinnitus?

Possibly, in some cases. Tinnitus, such as ringing or buzzing in the ears, has many possible causes. In some people it is influenced by the muscles and joints of the jaw and neck, which is sometimes called somatic tinnitus. If your tinnitus changes when you move your jaw or neck, clench, feel muscle tension or hold certain postures, a jaw and neck assessment may be worthwhile.

We can check whether your jaw or neck seem to be contributing and, if they do, focus your treatment there. Not all tinnitus is linked to the jaw or neck, and we cannot promise it will improve. If your ear symptoms are new, ongoing or worrying, please see your doctor or call the nurse line on 811 first, so an ear problem can be ruled out.

What should I do if my jaw is locked shut?

If you can close your mouth but cannot open it normally, do not force it. Pulling, stretching or testing the jaw again and again can make the muscles guard harder and the jaw more irritated. Instead:

  • Rest it. For a day or two, stick to foods that need very little chewing, such as soup, eggs, yogurt, pasta, soft rice and fish. Avoid gum, steak, raw vegetables, large sandwiches and chewy bread.
  • Keep it relaxed. When you are not eating, let your lips rest together and your teeth stay slightly apart. Do not clench, do not open as wide as you can, and support your jaw with your hand when you yawn.
  • Try heat or cold. Use whichever feels better on the sore area for short periods, with a cloth between the pack and your skin.
  • Check any medication. Take an over-the-counter pain reliever only if it is safe for you, and follow the label or your doctor's advice.

A brief catch that releases right away and leaves your jaw moving normally can usually be watched. If your jaw stays limited, or keeps locking, call your doctor or the nurse line (see below), and book a TMJ assessment so we can work out why it is happening. This is general information and does not replace an in-person assessment.

When should I call my doctor or the nurse line?

Call your doctor, or HealthLink BC's nurse line at 811, if:

  • your jaw stays locked, or you cannot open it about two finger-widths
  • you cannot eat or drink comfortably, or your pain is severe or getting worse
  • your bite suddenly feels different
  • the locking keeps coming back
  • a click you used to have has stopped and your jaw now opens less
  • this is your first unexplained lock and it has not released

Go to the emergency department, or call 911, if your mouth is stuck open and will not close, if you have trouble breathing, swallowing or managing your saliva, if the problem began after a fall, blow or accident, or if you have fast-growing swelling in your face or neck, fever with swelling or dental pain, new facial weakness or numbness, trouble speaking, vision changes, or a sudden severe headache. If your mouth is stuck open, support your jaw gently with your hand and do not try to force it back.
